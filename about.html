About OpenedCog

Hey, parents.

Raise your hand if this sounds familiar. You receive a letter from your kid’s school. The letter informs you that some standardized assessment or “screening” will be administered, perhaps quite soon, and that it will measure your child’s aptitude. Depending on the scores, your child might be placed in a gifted program. And, just maybe, your kid is young enough that they’ve never even taken a formal assessment before, much less a test on a computer over the course of multiple days.

Letter in hand, maybe you’re now wondering: What’s this test going to ask my kid? How does this test measure aptitude? Why does my school district use this to screen for whatever gifted means? Can my kid even sit still and focus long enough to complete the assessment? Maybe we should opt out?

This was our family’s circumstance, and these were some of the questions we asked ourselves, when we learned about a month into this school year that our seven-year-old son would be administered the Cognitive Abilities Test (CogAT) Level 8. All second-grade students at our son’s public elementary school were to be screened in an “aptitude assessment” in order to “determine eligibility for gifted identification.” We received the letter on a Friday afternoon, it was crumpled at the bottom of his backpack, and in reading it we learned that the assessment was to be administered in about two weeks. The letter was mostly bureaucratic and raised more questions than it answered. But no worries, for we were told: “Please encourage your child to relax and do their best. Plenty of sleep and a healthy breakfast may be helpful before testing.”

Here’s the thing—I’ve long struggled with formal academic assessment, whether as a student myself, as a former public school teacher, as an educational researcher, and now as a parent.

When I was a student in high school, I spent thousands of dollars—which I earned stocking shelves and sweeping floors at a local grocer—to enroll in an SAT prep course. It didn’t help, my score stunk, and the money was wasted. Thank goodness I attended a liberal arts college that placed little value on such standardized measures of intelligence or capability.

Twenty years ago, when I taught middle school in the South Bronx, my students were ill-prepared to take New York State’s standardized exams in mathematics and literacy. Part of that was my fault—I was a novice educator, I lacked pedagogical competence, and I failed to focus my students’ collective energies and motivation on such a formal assessment. But it wasn’t just my instructional shortcomings. The exam was biased. I recall a series of reading comprehension questions associated with a passage about an orchestra. Most of the questions asked about the conductor. Of course, the only “conductor” my students were familiar with was the individual who drove the subway train. My students, in general, didn’t perform well on the exam and I know that further impeded their educational pathways.

For more than a decade, I’ve worked as an educational researcher and I know that assessments like the CogAT are limited—by design—to capture but a narrow sliver of childhood intellect and ability, to say nothing of curiosity and creativity. I also know that these assessments become tools that entrench educational inequality.

You don’t need to be an educational researcher like me to know that “gifted” and “talented” and “aptitude” and “smart” are all social constructs. These are just ideas that reflect dominant culture biases about what knowledge is, whose intelligence counts, how knowing may be competently demonstrated, and what then happens when measures of “gifted” are used to sort and stratify our children. The weight of such assessment burdens the small shoulders of our kids. And that is both deeply problematic and, often, quite unfair.

If, as a parent, your family has been in this situation, maybe you were concerned, or confused, or maybe you were simply resigned to the realities of such testing and either threw your hands in the air or just shrugged. I imagine, however you felt, that you hugged your kid, reassured them, and made sure they slept well and ate a healthy breakfast.

In our family, something surprising happened. That Friday evening, about ten minutes after we pulled the letter from our son’s backpack to read and talk with him, my wife turned to me and asked, “Why don’t we just ask Claude?” You see, over the past nine months I’ve been regularly using agentic AI to build a variety of personal and professional projects. Why not build a little kid-friendly app to help our son prep for the CogAT Level 8? So that’s exactly what I did, and that’s why OpenedCog now exists.

The initial build took Claude just about 15 minutes. I shared a photo of the letter we received, wrote a few prompts, and Claude was off and running. Our son started what we called “puzzle practice” the very next day. Over the course of about two weeks, Claude and I made a few feature refinements because our son’s use provided immediate feedback on the design. For the sake of transparency, here’s what we did as a family as recorded by the app and documented by Claude: “Between September 19 and October 5, NAME completed 37 puzzle sets over 14 short practice sittings. He worked through all nine subtests in learning mode, then took about three rounds of pretend test days.” Each puzzle set took about three minutes, and a whole sitting rarely ran past fifteen.

Of course, this was never about prediction, some hoped for score, or the fallacy that our family’s app could secure placement for our son in our public school’s gifted program. Rather, this was about familiarity with the format. My wife and I didn’t want our son sitting for three “batteries” of an assessment lacking familiarity with how the puzzles would look, what directions he’d receive, and what types of problems he’d be expected to solve. That core commitment—familiarity—drove Claude’s design. Because here’s what practice can and can’t do as noted by Claude:

  • The goal is familiarity with the format: how the puzzles look, how the directions sound, and how to move through a test. That way a child's score reflects their reasoning, not surprise or nerves.
  • Practice scores do not predict a child's percentile, score, or placement.
  • Keep sessions short and stop while it's still fun. This is practice for a young child, not a drill.

At some point during the 37 puzzle sets that our son completed, I realized it would be necessary to share some version of our family’s app with the world. There are too many well-documented social advantages and financial privileges that some families wield to ensure their children benefit from the established stratification of American education. Tutoring. Private schools. Access to and use of learning technologies including, increasingly, AI. Creating an openly accessible version of what I designed for my son won’t materially disrupt deep educational, social, and economic inequalities that compound for our most vulnerable children. But, perhaps, your family or another will come across OpenedCog and use it, and perhaps also improve it (for the code is open), so that your second-grader is a bit more prepared to take the CogAT Level 8.

In deciding to code and share OpenedCog, Claude and I identified and enacted six guiding principles that operated as filters on every decision:

  1. Honesty: Because we can’t promise that practice predicts or improves a child's result.
  2. Access and accessibility: A free, no-account site for all families, checked against WCAG 2.2 accessibility standards.
  3. Ease of use: Families of any technical skill can start in under a minute.
  4. Privacy: OpenedCog collects nothing and your kid’s progress lives only on your family's device.
  5. Openness: The code is public and the content is openly licensed.
  6. The child's experience comes first: This is practice, not a drill.

If you’re interested, read the design principles in detail because it shows how a parent (me) and an algorithm (Claude) worked to create something tangible that may be of use to other families.

Our son took the CogAT two weeks ago. As I write this, we’re still waiting on his scores. But, regardless of the outcome, this whole process has raised some rather interesting and troubling questions:

  • As a parent, will I continually use AI to create just-in-time apps and micro learning activities to better prepare my son for the many assessment hoops he’ll be asked to jump through?
  • Then again, at what point will my son start making those very apps for himself?
  • With OpenedCog as a proof of concept, how will I continue to use my technical skills and educational know-how to help other families navigate challenging and ambiguous circumstances?
  • How are other parents creating small-scale and privacy-forward AI solutions for their own children?
  • How might this project nudge other families away from purchasing AI products that infringe upon a child’s privacy?
  • If parents are designing and using these types of AI apps with their children, should debate about academic integrity be reframed as a multi-generational concern?
  • In making the OpenedCog app and code base open, might I potentially widen educational disparities because of the types of families who may most likely find, use, and improve this type of technology?

If your family is facing the CogAT Level 8, I do hope OpenedCog relieves a bit of stress and helps your kid, even minimally, gain some familiarity with this standardized assessment. And please remember this: Your child’s many gifts and talents will never be meaningfully measured by any test that fundamentally constrains the true beauty and diversity of children’s intelligence, curiosity, and wisdom.

- Remi, parent, educator, and Claude collaborator, October 19, 2026
