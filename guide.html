1. Start here

The goal of practice is simple: on test day, nothing about the format should surprise your child. That way, the score reflects how they reason, not whether they understood the buttons, the voice, or what a question type was asking.

The CogAT is a reasoning test, not a knowledge test. You can't teach the answers, and drilling won't build reasoning. Three things do help:

  • knowing what each of the nine question types asks
  • a few calm habits: listen to the whole question, look at every choice, and guess rather than skip
  • enough stamina to stay careful through a long sitting

What OpenedCog can't do. Every puzzle here is original. None are real test items. Practice scores don't convert to CogAT scores and can't predict your child's result or placement. No service or technology can honestly promise that practice raises a score. Familiarity is the goal, not a number.

The five-minute version

  1. Open OpenedCog together and tap How the test works. Let it read to your child.
  2. For the first few sessions, use Practice one kind of puzzle (learning mode). Your child gets feedback and an explanation after every answer.
  3. Once they've tried all nine types, switch to Pretend test day. It works like the real test: no feedback until the end.
  4. After every session, tap Look at the ones to fix and go over the misses together.
  5. Every few days, check the Grown-ups page to see which puzzle types need more time.
  6. Keep it light. Call it "puzzle practice," not something like "the gifted test."

2. What the real test is like

OpenedCog is built around CogAT Level 8, the level most schools give in second grade. Districts give the test in different ways, so treat this section as the general picture and check the details with your school (see the next section).

WhatAt Level 8
ReadingNone required. Every question and answer is a picture, and the questions are read aloud.
Who reads aloudOnline, a recorded voice reads through headphones, in English or Spanish. Some schools instead have a teacher read the questions aloud.
TimingUntimed. Riverside, the publisher, estimates about 11 to 15 minutes per subtest, but children aren't stopped by a clock.
LengthNine subtests in three batteries. Each subtest has 14 to 18 questions.
ScheduleRiverside recommends no more than one battery (three subtests) per day at this level, so the full test is often spread over three days.
Practice questionsEach subtest opens with practice questions. Online, a wrong practice answer gets a spoken explanation, and the child must choose the right one to move on.
Buttons (online)Go moves ahead, Back goes to the previous question, a replay button repeats the question, and Done ends the subtest. After Done, a child can't return to that subtest. Some buttons are unavailable while the voice is playing.

The three batteries

Battery (OpenedCog's name)Subtests
Verbal (Picture puzzles)Picture Analogies, Sentence Completion, Picture Classification
Quantitative (Number puzzles)Number Analogies, Number Puzzles, Number Series
Nonverbal (Shape puzzles)Figure Matrices, Paper Folding, Figure Classification

Some school districts use a shorter Screening Form. It has just the three analogy subtests: Picture Analogies, Number Analogies, and Figure Matrices. Children who do well may then take the other six. If your district uses it, those three practice tiles matter most.

OpenedCog models the online, read-aloud version, because that's the one with buttons to learn. If your child's school uses paper and a teacher reading aloud, the puzzle types are the same and the practice still applies. Just skip the button lessons.

3. Questions to ask your school

Schools usually send a letter before screening. If it doesn't answer these, the person to ask is often your child's teacher or the school's gifted or advanced learning contact.

  • When is testing, and over how many days?
  • Is it the full CogAT or the Screening Form?
  • Online or on paper? On what device: a laptop, a Chromebook, or a tablet?
  • Should my child bring headphones?
  • Can my child take the audio in Spanish?
  • If my child has an IEP or 504 plan, will its accommodations apply?
  • How will the scores be used, and when will we get results?
  • Can families opt out, and is there a deadline?

4. Setting up OpenedCog (ten minutes, grown-ups only)

Do this before your child sits down, so the first session is all puzzles and no fiddling.

  1. Pick the device. Practice on the closest match to what your child will test on. A mouse or trackpad feels different from tapping a screen.
  2. Use a browser that reads aloud well. Chrome, Safari, and Edge work best. Read-aloud voices in Firefox are often weaker.
  3. Plug in headphones if your child will wear them at school.
  4. On an iPhone or iPad, add OpenedCog to your Home Screen (Share button, then "Add to Home Screen"). Safari erases a site's saved progress after seven days of using Safari without visiting it. Opening OpenedCog from the Home Screen avoids that.
  5. Type your child's first name or a nickname on the welcome screen. It stays on this device.
  6. Open Grown-ups. Tap Grown-ups and answer the multiplication question. It changes each time, so a young child can't memorize it.
  7. Choose the starting settings:
    • Puzzles per part on test day: 8 at first, then 12, then 16 at least once (the real subtests run 14 to 18).
    • Puzzles in a learning set: 8 when a type is new. 12 or 16 to check whether it has stuck.
    • Read-aloud voice: On.
    • Voice speed: Normal. Choose Slow if your child replays a lot.
    • Show the spoken question as text: Yes at first. Turn it off a week or so before the test, since the real test doesn't show it.
  8. Tap Test the voice. If you hear nothing, see Troubleshooting.
  9. Tap Save settings.

Where progress is saved. Only in your browser, on your device. Nothing is sent anywhere. Clearing browsing data, using a private window, or switching devices or browsers starts fresh. To keep progress safe or move it, use Save a backup file on the Grown-ups page, and load it on the other device with Load a backup file. The file holds your children's names and practice results, so keep it private.

More than one child? Tap Add another child under the greeting on the home screen. Each child has their own history and settings. To switch, tap Not [name]? Switch.

5. A tour of the app

Home screen

  • Pretend test day has three cards, one per battery: Picture puzzles (Verbal), Number puzzles (Quantitative), and Shape puzzles (Nonverbal).
  • Practice one kind of puzzle has nine tiles, one per subtest. Each tile shows a kid-friendly name, the official subtest name, and stars for the best score so far: one star at 40%, two at 70%, three at 90%.
  • How the test works reads a one-minute explanation of the buttons and three test-taking habits.
  • Grown-ups opens your progress page and settings.

The puzzle screen

  • Hear it again replays the question. Encourage your child to use it. The real test has a replay button too.
  • Answer choices A to D. Tap a picture to choose it. Your child can change their mind. On a keyboard, A, B, C, and D, or 1, 2, 3, and 4, also work, and Enter checks and moves on.
  • Go stays gray while the voice is reading, as some buttons do on the real test.
  • Back returns to the previous puzzle in test-day mode.
  • Done appears on the last puzzle. OpenedCog asks "Are you all done?" because the real test won't allow going back afterward.
  • Stop leaves the session. In test-day mode, an unfinished part isn't saved.

After a session

The results screen shows the score and stars for each part. Look at the ones to fix shows every miss: the puzzle, what your child picked, the right answer, and a Hear why button.

The number, bead, shape, and paper-folding puzzles are new every time. The three picture puzzle types draw from fixed sets of about 45 to 60 puzzles each. Unseen puzzles come first, so repeats start only after many sessions.

6. The nine puzzle types, and how to coach them

The nine subtests come in three families. Once your child understands one member of a family, the others get easier.

  • Pairs (analogies): Picture, Number, and Shape pairs. "How does the top pair go together? Do the same thing to the bottom."
  • Groups (classification): Picture and Shape groups. "What is the same about all three?"
  • The rest: Listen and choose, Number sentences, Bead patterns, and Paper folding each work their own way.

In learning mode, ask the coaching question before your child taps, and let them answer out loud.

Subtest (OpenedCog's name)What your child doesCoaching questionCommon trap
Picture Analogies (Picture pairs)Finds how two pictures relate, then applies it to a new picture"Say the top pair as a sentence. Now finish that sentence for the bottom."Picking something that just goes with the bottom picture, not the relationship
Sentence Completion (Listen and choose)Hears a question and picks the picture that answers it"What did the question ask for? Say it back to me."Missing words like NOT, AND, or MOST, which change the answer
Picture Classification (Picture groups)Finds what three pictures share, then picks one that shares it too"What is the same about all three? Does your answer have that too?"Choosing something merely related, like headphones next to instruments
Number Analogies (Number pairs)Counts two boxes, finds the change (more, fewer, double, half), and applies it"How many on the left? How many on the right? What happened?"Miscounting, or matching the object instead of the number
Number Puzzles (Number sentences)Finds the missing number that makes both sides of = equal"What does the finished side add up to? What does the other side need?"Answering with the total of the first side. In 4 + 3 = 5 + ?, the answer is 2, not 7.
Number Series (Bead patterns)Counts the beads on each string and finds what comes next"Count each string out loud. What's the pattern?"Assuming it always goes up by one. Some go down, repeat, or zigzag.
Figure Matrices (Shape pairs)Sees how one shape changed, then changes a new shape the same way"What changed: color, size, how many, which way it points, or a dot?"Catching one change and missing a second
Paper Folding (Paper folding)Pictures where punched holes land when the paper is unfolded"How many layers did the hole go through? Where does each one land when you open it?"Unfolding only once when the paper was folded twice
Figure Classification (Shape groups)Finds the one thing three shapes share"Some things are different on purpose. What's the same on all three?"Grabbing the answer that looks most like one of the three

Try real paper for Paper Folding. It's the type of puzzle most young children have never seen. Fold a sheet of paper in half, poke a hole with a pencil, and ask your child to predict before you open it. Then fold it twice. Five minutes of this often makes the idea click faster than the on-screen puzzle does.

Three habits for every type

  1. Listen to the whole question before looking at the answers.
  2. Look at every answer before choosing. The first one that looks right is often a trap.
  3. Stuck? Make a best guess. Never leave one blank.

7. Running a practice session

A good session lasts 15 to 25 minutes, ends while your child still wants more, and has a grown-up nearby.

Before

  • Pick a time when your child is fed and fresh. After a snack beats right before bed.
  • Choose one plan: one Pretend test day, or two or three practice tiles. Not both.
  • Headphones on, and your phone away. Children take their cues from us.

During learning mode: coach actively

  • Before your child taps, ask the coaching question for that type.
  • If they miss one, let the app explain first. Then ask, "Can you tell me why that one is right?"
  • Praise the process, not the result: "You checked all four answers. That's the right strategy."

During test-day mode: sit on your hands

  • No hints, no pointing, and no reaction to answers. A proctor can't help with questions, so neither should you.
  • You can help with the mechanics: which button to press, or how to replay a question.
  • Watch how your child works and note it for later. Do they rush? Skip the replay? Stare at one answer?

After (two to five minutes)

  • Tap Look at the ones to fix.
  • For each miss, let your child look again before you tap Hear why. Children often catch their own mistake on a second look.
  • Ask them to explain the right answer in their own words.
  • End on something they got right.

What to say, and what to skip

SaySkip
"These are tricky puzzles. Grown-ups get them wrong too.""This test decides if you're gifted."
"Nice checking. You looked at every answer.""You're so smart."
"What's your rule?""No, it's C." (In learning mode, let the app explain.)
"Let's stop here and do more tomorrow.""Just one more," when they're done

When frustration shows up

Stop. Don't push through. Switch to a type your child likes, or end with a round of real-paper folding. Frustration in practice teaches a child to dread the test, which costs more than a skipped session.

Watch for fatigue late in a long sitting

A dip at the end of a long session usually means tiredness, not a gap in understanding. Before drawing conclusions, try that puzzle type again fresh on another day. The real test is long too, and building stamina gently, with one session at 16 puzzles per part, helps more than extra sessions.

8. A sample plan

Adjust this to your calendar and your child. It introduces all nine types in the first three sessions, rehearses each battery, and tapers before the test. Each session is about 20 minutes.

Leave rest days between sessions where you can. Ten sessions spread over two weeks is plenty.

If the test is a month or more away

Do sessions 1 to 6 over a week or two, then switch to maintenance: two short sessions a week, alternating one learning set with one pretend test day. More than that won't strengthen reasoning, and it risks wearing your child out before test week.

The days just before the test

Keep the last session short and easy, a day or two before. Do no practice the night before or the morning of. A good night's sleep does more than one more round.

9. Reading the Grown-ups page

Check it every few days. It tells you where to spend the next session, not how your child will score.

  • Runs: how many sets your child has finished for that subtest, in either mode.
  • Last and Best: percent correct. In learning mode, only first tries count.
  • Recent: bars for the last eight runs. You're hoping to see them rise or hold steady.
  • Avg per puzzle: average seconds per puzzle over the last five runs.
  • Recent sessions: one row per set. The small squares show each puzzle in order, filled for right and hollow for wrong. On a computer, hover over a square to see its time and replays. A drop of 25 points or more from the first half of a set to the second is flagged, which can mean tiredness.

What to do with what you see

This is generic guidance based on how the app works, not research-based cutoffs or recommendations specific to your child.

What you seeLikely meaningWhat to try
Below about 60% after two learning runsThe type hasn't clicked yetAnother learning run, asking the coaching question out loud. For folding, use real paper.
Much lower in test-day mode than in learning modeFormat or focus, not understandingReinforce "look at every answer" and "use Hear it again." Watch for rushing.
Very fast times (well under 10 seconds) plus missesRushingHave your child say the rule out loud before tapping.
Very slow times (over 90 seconds) on some puzzlesGetting stuckPractice "make your best guess and move on."

Keep the scores in perspective. These are practice scores on original puzzles. OpenedCog's scores don't convert to CogAT percentiles, and they can't predict your child's result.

10. Test week and test day

In test week, the job shifts from practice to protecting sleep, mood, and confidence.

Before testing starts

  • Put the test days on the family calendar.
  • Plan normal bedtimes and real breakfasts.
  • If your child needs headphones, check that they work, are charged if necessary, and fit.
  • If your child has an IEP or 504 plan, confirm with the school that its accommodations will apply.

How to explain it

Keep it matter-of-fact: "For the next few mornings, your class is doing puzzles, just like the ones we practiced. Everybody does them. Take your time and do your best."

On each test morning

  • Normal routine, a real breakfast, and on time to school.
  • Remind your child of the three habits: listen to the whole question, look at every answer, and guess instead of skipping.
  • Remind them there's no timer, and that they can replay a question as often as they like.

After each test day

Ask "Which puzzles were fun?" rather than "How did you do?" Young children are poor judges of their own performance, and quizzing them adds pressure for the next day.

After testing

Each school district uses CogAT scores in its own way, often alongside other measures such as an achievement test or teacher input. Your school can explain what your child's results mean and what, if anything, comes next.

12. Quick reference card

Print this section or keep it on the fridge.

Every session

  • 15 to 25 minutes, fed and fresh, headphones on.
  • One plan: a pretend test day, or two or three practice tiles.
  • Learning mode: ask the coaching question before your child taps.
  • Test-day mode: no hints. Help with buttons only.
  • Always finish with Look at the ones to fix. Your child explains the answer back.
  • Stop before they're tired. End on a win.

Three habits

  1. Listen to the whole question. Use Hear it again.
  2. Look at every answer before choosing.
  3. Not sure? Best guess. Never blank.

Coaching questions

  • Pairs: "Say the top pair as a sentence. Now finish it for the bottom."
  • Groups: "What is the same about ALL three?"
  • Listen and choose: "What did the question ask for?"
  • Number sentences: "What does the finished side add up to?"
  • Bead patterns: "Count each string out loud. What's the pattern?"
  • Paper folding: "How many layers did the hole go through?"

Remember

Practice builds familiarity, not a score. No practice the night before. Ask "Which puzzles were fun?"
